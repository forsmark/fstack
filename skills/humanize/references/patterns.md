# Patterns

The rules a check applies, in four parts:

1. How to read.
2. Patterns of generated writing. Density decides which of these are findings: see step 4 of `SKILL.md`.
3. General rules for reader copy. Each one is a finding at one occurrence.
4. What a rewrite reaches for.

Each rule has a bad example and a better one. The examples come from made-up products. Match a string by what it does, not by its words: the example phrases are incomplete. The one exception is the list of overused words, which is taken literally.

The profile can change a rule where the rule says so. Where it says nothing, the default here applies.

## 1. How to read

**Whole.** Judge a page or a mail as its reader meets it, top to bottom, and not one string at a time. Most of these patterns show only across neighbouring strings.

**Density.** No pattern of part 2 proves anything alone, and each one also occurs in human writing. What gives copy away is density, and the same shape coming back.

**Not patterns.** Do not flag these:

- correct grammar;
- a plain, formal or dry tone;
- a mix of casual and formal;
- a transition word on its own ("however", "also");
- the presence or absence of a dash.

## 2. Patterns of generated writing

### Question, then answer

A sentence asks a question and the next one answers it. As a house style this is the loudest tell.

- Bad: "Forgot your password? Enter your email and we send a reset link."
- Better: "Enter your email to get a reset link."

Not this pattern: a heading that is a question, an entry in a list of questions (an FAQ), or a control whose whole label is a question.

### Paired rhythm

Two sentences side by side, each a condition followed by its result, with the same rhythm. No question mark is needed.

- Bad: "Signed in, your loans show here. Signed out, you can still search."
- Better: "Anyone can search the catalogue. Sign in and your loans show here as well."

Join the two into one sentence, or reword one of them.

### One shape, one length

Neighbouring strings built alike, such as three hints that each start with a verb and stop at eight words. Or a run of short sentences of about one length, each with one comma. Human writing varies: a long sentence, a three-word one, an aside.

- Bad, three hints under a booking form: "Pick a time that suits you. Bring the bike reasonably clean. Arrive five minutes early."
- Better: "Arrive five minutes early, with the bike reasonably clean." The first hint goes, because the form already asks for a time.

Break the run: join two sentences, cut one short, or remove one.

### Correcting a belief nobody held

A string sets up a claim only to correct it, to sound bigger: "not just X, but Y", "it's not an X, it's a Y", "no X, no Y, just Z".

- Bad: "Not just a reading list. A home for every book you love."
- Better: "Your reading list."

Not this pattern: a plain negation that gives the reader a fact they need, such as "The app shows no ads" or "Returns are free".

### A padded or cut list

A list made to hold three things, by padding it or by leaving something out. Three adjectives in a row is the common case.

- Bad: "Fast, simple and secure."
- Better: say what is there: "You pay in your bank's own app."

A list of three is fine when there are three separate things the reader gets or does.

### Selling

An advertiser's voice, or an ordinary thing made to sound large: "in one place", "everything you need", "never miss", "the home of", "a new chapter", "before it's too late", "discover".

- Bad: "Never miss a due date again."
- Better: "The app reminds you two days before a loan is due."

Replace the line with the fact it stands for, or remove it.

### A tail, a closing line, a colon that reveals

Three ways a sentence keeps going after its fact ends:

- A tail clause that comments on the fact and adds none, often "-ing", "so" or "which means": "We send a reminder two days before, so you never miss a return." Cut the tail.
- A closing line that sums up or looks ahead: "That's it, you're all set." "Either way, your notes are safe." Cut the line. A short sign-off that is the unit's one allowed beat ("See you on Tuesday.") is a beat, judged under part 3, not a closing line.
- A colon held back for effect: "Your receipt has one thing that matters: the order number." Better: "Keep the order number from your receipt." A colon that starts a list, a time or an address is fine.

### Dressed-up verbs and overused words

"serves as", "stands as", "features", "offers", "boasts", "provides", "allows you to", "lets you", where "is", "has" or the plain verb would do.

- Bad: "This page lets you manage your holds."
- Better: "Cancel or move a hold here."

Words that generated writing overuses. Take the list literally, not its synonyms: additionally, align with, bolster, crucial, delve, elevate, emphasize, enduring, enhance, foster, garner, highlight (as a verb), intricate, interplay, key (as an adjective), landscape, leverage, meticulous, multifaceted, pivotal, seamless, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore, valuable, vibrant. Replace the word with the plain one, or cut the sentence.

### A caveat said again

A reassurance or caveat belongs only where a reader would hesitate, and once on a page or in a mail.

- Bad: every step of a setup flow ends "You can change this later in Settings."
- Better: one step says it, the one where the choice feels final.

### An assistant's voice

Copy that talks to the reader the way a chat assistant does: "Here's what happens next", "Good news:", "Heads up", "Just so you know", "We've got you covered", "Let us know if there's anything else".

- Bad: "Good news: your order has shipped."
- Better: "Your order has shipped."

Cut the opener and start with the fact. Cut an offer of help at the end. Where a contact is a fact the reader needs, state it plainly: "Questions about this order go to the address below."

### A beat as a template

A beat is a light line: a joke, a wink, a short punchy fragment.

- The same kind of beat on every message of one kind is a template. "Even bikes need a rest." after one error is a voice. After every error it is a template. Keep at most one, where it reads best.
- A short fragment after a longer sentence, there only to land it: "Your notes stay on your device. Always." Better: "Your notes stay on your device."
- Even cheerfulness: one friendly temperature in an error, a deleted account and a welcome mail alike.

### Vague connection

"related to", "associated with", "connected to", "around", where the plain relation can be said.

- Bad: "Problems related to your account."
- Better: "Problems signing in."

### Formatting habits

These count only when they are dense:

- Title Case In Headings;
- bold scattered through prose;
- a bold lead-in on every list item;
- an emoji at the start of a heading.

### Borrowed weight

"Loved by readers everywhere", "experts agree", "many people say", or a famous name dropped to borrow weight. Cut the claim, or name the source and what it said.

## 3. General rules for reader copy

### A string earns its place

A string says something the reader needs at that point and does not already have from the label, heading or control beside it. A string that only repeats its neighbour is removed, not shortened.

- Bad: a checkbox "Email me when a hold is ready", with the hint "We'll email you when your hold is ready."
- Better: the checkbox alone.

Removing a string never removes a sentence that something else requires: why a mail arrived, how to stop it and what stopping it leaves, a legal notice, a line the profile marks as required.

### A string names no machinery

A string says what the reader sees or can do, and never names the machinery behind it: a session, a request, a status code, a URL, a database, a job, a queue, a cache, a token. The profile lists the project's own internal words. Use the reader's word for each one, and use it the same way everywhere.

- Bad: "Session expired (401). Please re-authenticate."
- Better: "You were signed out. Sign in again to carry on."

Technical readers get the same rule. They read what happened faster than how it happened.

### A failure is told in the reader's words

A page shows no status code, no parameter name and no message written for another program. Where the page has words of its own for the failure, it uses them and says what to do. Where it has none, it says that something went wrong and that the reader can try again. Where a load failed, "Try again" is a control beside the words.

- Bad: "Error: invalid value for field 'date'."
- Better: "That date has passed. Pick a later one."

### A time limit is a number

Where a string sets a time limit the reader acts against (a link or code that stops working, a hold, a deadline, a window to cancel), it states the limit as a number: "The link works once, for 15 minutes." "Soon", "shortly" and "for a limited time" are findings.

In code, the number comes from the same value that sets the limit, and is never typed by hand into the string. Check this where the code is in reach. In pasted text, say it was not checked. Where the copy does not hold the number, a rewrite writes a placeholder and asks the owner for the value.

### You, and we

Copy calls the reader "you". "We" means the people who run the product, and covers what they do or decide, by hand or by their systems: "We send you a link", "We delete your data within 30 days". A failure is not told as "we": "We couldn't load your files" turns an error into an apology, so say "Your files could not be loaded" and what to do. Copy says "I" nowhere.

The profile can set a stricter rule, such as "we" only where the copy asks the reader to tell the team something, or a different one, such as a product written in one person's voice.

### One light beat at most

A string carries one beat at most, and only where a reader who misses it loses nothing: an empty state, a confirmation, a tip, a page that does not exist, an error. The beat comes after the fact, never in its place. An error says first, in plain words, what went wrong and what to do. A beat the reader has to decode is cut.

The profile says which places may carry a beat. Without a profile, the places named above may.

### Where play stops

These carry no beat:

- copy about privacy, consent, unsubscribing, payment or deleting an account;
- a mail about the account or its address;
- a status and a date, which are stated plainly.

Legal and consent text keeps its meaning wherever its tone changes.

### Exclamation marks

Copy holds no exclamation mark. The profile can allow them.

## 4. What a rewrite reaches for

Copy scrubbed of everything below reads as written by a machine or a lawyer. Reach for these when a sentence needs new words:

- plain "is" and "has" sentences;
- the plain word over the stiff one: "wrote", not "authored"; "used", not "utilized";
- a qualifier where it is true: "usually", "mostly", "a bit";
- a definite claim where it is true: "the only", "the first";
- sentences of different lengths.
